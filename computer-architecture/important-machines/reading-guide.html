Computer architecture · Important machines & ISAs

Landmark computers and instruction sets, told through their system-description and retrospective papers — the machines that fixed the vocabulary and the ISAs that endured. The path below is conceptual, not chronological: start with what an architecture even is (System/360), then watch the two abstractions every later machine leans on — virtual memory and in-CPU parallelism — get invented; then follow the great ISA debates (stack vs. vector, mini vs. mainframe, RISC vs. CISC) down to the microprocessors that won, and out to the open, compiler-scheduled, and free ISAs that argue over what comes next. Techniques these machines pioneered are treated in depth in the mechanism subtopics; here the focus is the machine as a whole.

How to read this list. Keep one distinction in mind throughout: the architecture (the instruction set a programmer sees) versus the microarchitecture (how a particular chip implements it). System/360 made that line explicit; the RISC papers argue about where to draw it; and the x86 chapters show a single architecture outliving a dozen wildly different implementations.

1 · What an architecture is — the durable contract

Start here: an instruction set is a contract you keep across many implementations, fast and slow.

  1. Architecture of the IBM System/360 — Amdahl, Blaauw, Brooks
    PDF·15ppIBM J. R&D 1964 · DOI · PDF
    ISA-as-durable-contract: one architecture spanning a whole line of models, from cheap to fast. The founding statement of the idea.
  2. Design Objectives for the IBM Stretch Computer — S. W. Dunwell
    DOI·3ppEastern JCC 1957 · DOI
    The ambitious 7030 whose overreach and lessons — pipelining, instruction lookahead, interrupts, the 8-bit byte — fed directly into System/360.
    • PDFThe Engineering Design of the Stretch Computer — Erich Bloch 11pp. The companion engineering account of how Stretch was actually built. PDF

2 · Making memory an abstraction

Next, the two tricks that let every later machine pretend it has more memory than it does — and faster memory than it has.

  1. One-Level Storage System — Kilburn, Edwards, Lanigan, Sumner
    PDF·13ppIRE Trans. Electronic Computers 1962 · DOI · PDF
    The Manchester Atlas paging scheme — the first practical virtual memory, and the origin of the term.
  2. Structural Aspects of the System/360 Model 85, II: The Cache — J. S. Liptay
    PDF·7ppIBM Systems J. 1968 · DOI · PDF
    The first commercial machine with a cache — the other memory illusion, making a large slow store look small and fast, invisibly to software.

3 · Parallelism inside one processor

  1. Parallel Operation in the Control Data 6600 — J. E. Thornton
    PDF·8ppAFIPS FJCC 1964 · DOI · PDF
    The first scoreboarded out-of-order supercomputer and an archetypal load/store design — Seymour Cray's breakthrough machine.
  2. The IBM System/360 Model 91: Machine Philosophy and Instruction-Handling — Anderson, Sparacio, Tomasulo
    PDF·17ppIBM J. R&D 1967 · DOI · PDF

6 · The RISC revolution

The reaction to CISC complexity: small instruction sets, deep pipelines, and work pushed onto the compiler — from research prototype to commercial big iron to a free, open ISA.

  1. The 801 Minicomputer — G. Radin
    PDF·9ppASPLOS-I 1982 · DOI · PDF
    IBM's 801 — the progenitor of RISC and of compiler-driven load/store design.
  2. A VLSI RISC (Berkeley RISC-I) — Patterson, Séquin
    DOI·14ppIEEE Computer 1982 · DOI
    Reduced instruction set, register windows, and the single-chip VLSI economics that made it pay.
    • PDFRISC I: A Reduced Instruction Set VLSI Computer — Patterson, Séquin 15pp. The original architecture paper, openly available. PDF
  3. MIPS: A Microprocessor Architecture — Hennessy, Jouppi, Przybylski, Rowen, Gross, Baskett, Gill
    PDF·6ppMICRO-15 1982 · DOI · PDF

  4. The Scalable Processor Architecture (SPARC) — Garner, Agrawal, Briggs, Brown, Hough, Joy, Kleiman, Muchnick, Namjoo, Patterson, Pendleton, Tuck
    DOI·6ppCOMPCON 1988 · DOI
    Sun's open RISC ISA — register windows taken commercial, and designed as a licensable standard rather than one company's chip.
    • PDFThe SPARC Architecture Manual, Version 8 — SPARC International 295pp. The canonical, freely published ISA reference — a model of what "architecture as contract" looks like on paper. PDF
  5. Precision Architecture (PA-RISC) — Ruby B. Lee
    DOI·14ppIEEE Computer 1989 · DOI
    HP's commercial RISC, built to span an entire product line from minicomputers to mainframe-class systems — RISC as a durable corporate contract in the System/360 mold.

  6. IBM RISC System/6000 Processor Architecture — R. R. Oehler, R. D. Groves
    PDF·14ppIBM J. R&D 1990 · DOI · PDF
    POWER: the 801 idea grown into a multiple-issue commercial workstation CPU, and the direct ancestor of the PowerPC and today's POWER line.
    • PDFThe Evolution of RISC Technology at IBM — Cocke, Markstein 8pp. John Cocke's own account of the line from the 801 research machine to RS/6000. PDF
  7. The Acorn RISC Machine — An Architectural View — S. B. Furber, A. R. Wilson
    DOI·4ppElectronics & Power 1987 · DOI
    The original ARM: a tiny British RISC built for a low-cost PC, whose descendants now ship in the tens of billions.

  8. Instruction Sets Should Be Free: The Case for RISC-V — Asanović, Patterson
    PDF·7ppUC Berkeley EECS TR 2014 · PDF
    The RISC argument taken to its conclusion: an open, royalty-free, modular ISA that anyone can implement — the "architecture as contract" idea, made a public good.

7 · Microprocessors take over — x86 and the RISC high-water mark

From the first single-chip CPU to the ISAs that won the desktop and server, plus the clean-slate 64-bit bet that lost — and where single-thread performance peaked.

  1. The History of the 4004 — Faggin, Hoff, Mazor, Shima
    PDF·11ppIEEE Micro 1996 · DOI · PDF
    The designers' own account of the Intel 4004 — the first commercial single-chip microprocessor, and the seed of everything in this section.
  2. The Intel 8086: A 16-bit Evolution of the 8080 — Morse, Pohlman, Ravenel
    DOI·10ppIEEE Computer 1978 · DOI
    The origin of the x86 instruction set — still, decades later, the dominant desktop and server ISA.
    • PDFIntel Microprocessors: 8008 to 8086 — Stephen Morse et al 47pp. The lead designer's own detailed history of how the 8086 came to be. PDF
  3. Tuning the Pentium Pro Microarchitecture (P6) — David B. Papworth
    DOI·8ppIEEE Micro 1996 · DOI
    The x86 turning point: a CISC ISA run as a high-performance out-of-order core by decoding instructions into RISC-like μops.
    • WEBInside the P6 — Mark Smotherman (course notes) A concise walkthrough of the P6 pipeline and the μop idea. WEB
  4. The AMD Opteron Processor for Multiprocessor Servers — Keltcher, McGrath, Ahmed, Conway
    PDF·11ppIEEE Micro 2003 · DOI · PDF
    AMD64: the 64-bit extension that x86 actually kept, plus an on-chip memory controller and HyperTransport.
  5. Introducing the IA-64 Architecture — Huck, Morris, Ross, Knies, Mulder, Zahir
    PDF·12ppIEEE Micro 2000 · DOI · PDF
    The other 64-bit answer — a clean-slate HP/Intel ISA (Itanium) that bet on the compiler to expose parallelism explicitly (EPIC), predication, and speculation. The great counterexample: technically bold, commercially eclipsed by evolutionary AMD64.
    • PDFUnderstanding EPIC Architectures and Implementations — Mark Smotherman 8pp. A clear survey of what EPIC/VLIW promised and why in-order, compiler-scheduled parallelism struggled against out-of-order hardware. PDF
  6. The Alpha 21264 Microprocessor — R. E. Kessler
    PDF·13ppIEEE Micro 1999 · DOI · PDF
    An aggressive out-of-order superscalar RISC — a high-water mark for single-thread performance and a canonical microarchitecture case study.
Architecture outlives implementation. The through-line of this list is the split System/360 made explicit: an instruction set is a promise to software, while a microarchitecture is one hardware team's way of keeping it. Virtual memory (Atlas), caches (Model 85), and dynamic scheduling (6600, Model 91) are microarchitectural tricks hidden behind that promise. The RISC papers argue about how simple the promise should be so the hardware can go fast — and how open it should be, from Sun's licensable SPARC to the free RISC-V. IA-64 pushed the promise the other way, asking the compiler to schedule the parallelism explicitly, and lost to an evolutionary AMD64. The x86 chapters (4004 → 8086 → P6 → Opteron) show a deliberately un-simple promise surviving fifty years precisely because it was never tied to one implementation.
