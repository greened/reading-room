Computer architecture · Quantitative principles

Computer architecture became a quantitative discipline once performance, power, and area could be reasoned about with a few durable laws and models — Amdahl, Moore, Dennard, the memory wall, roofline, and the end-of-scaling arguments. This guide reads them as a path: first how we measure speed, then the scaling laws that made it free, the bounds on parallel speedup, the walls where the free lunch ran out, and finally the models architects use to design under those limits.

How to read this list. Start with the metric — the iron law, honest benchmarking, and the standard suites — because every later argument is really a statement about one of its terms. Then take the two exponentials (Moore, Dennard) that shrank those terms for free, the speedup bounds (Amdahl, Gustafson) that cap what parallelism buys, and the walls — off-chip bandwidth, cache misses, and wire delay — that, together with the end of Dennard scaling, ended the free lunch. Close with the working models (Little's Law, roofline, mechanistic) and the design arguments that quantitative reasoning settles.

Measuring performance: what "faster" means

You cannot argue about limits until you fix how performance is measured — and how honestly you summarize it.

  1. A Characterization of Processor Performance in the VAX-11/780 — Emer, Clark
    PDF·10ppISCA 1984 · DOI · PDF
    The measurement study behind the "iron law": execution time is instructions × cycles-per-instruction × cycle time, and you must measure all three.
  2. How Not to Lie with Statistics: The Correct Way to Summarize Benchmark Results — Fleming, Wallace
    DOI·4ppCACM 1986 · DOI
    Why the arithmetic mean of normalized results misleads and the geometric mean is the honest summary — the statistical basis of SPEC-style reporting.
  3. SPEC CPU2000: Measuring CPU Performance in the New Millennium — John Henning
    PDF·8ppIEEE Computer 2000 · DOI · PDF
    How the industry-standard suite is actually built and reported — the workloads, the reference machine, and the base/peak rules that turn the honest-summary principle into a repeatable methodology.
  4. The Case for Application-Specific Benchmarking — Seltzer, Krinsky, Smith, Zhang
    PDF·6ppHotOS 1999 · DOI · PDF
    The counterweight to a single suite score: it predicts little about the workload you actually run, so measure your application — or be misled.

The scaling laws that powered the free lunch

The two exponentials that gave four decades of "free" performance — and whose ends frame everything after.

  1. Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits — Gordon Moore
    PDF·4ppElectronics 1965 · DOI · PDF
    The observation that became Moore's Law: transistor count per chip doubles on a fixed cadence, setting the industry's whole rhythm.
  2. Design of Ion-Implanted MOSFETs with Very Small Physical Dimensions — Dennard, Gaensslen, Yu, Rideout, Bassous, LeBlanc
    PDF·11ppIEEE JSSC 1974 · DOI · PDF
    States Dennard scaling — shrink a transistor and power density holds constant — the device-physics rule whose end reshaped everything after.
  3. A 30 Year Retrospective on Dennard's MOSFET Scaling Paper — Mark Bohr
    PDF·5ppIEEE SSCS Newsletter 2007 · DOI · PDF
    A process technologist revisits which of Dennard's rules held and which broke — the bridge from the 1974 device physics to why frequency scaling stalled in the mid-2000s.

How far parallelism goes

Once one core isn't enough, how much can more of them actually buy?

  1. Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities — Gene Amdahl
    PDF·4ppAFIPS 1967 · DOI · PDF
    Origin of Amdahl's Law: a fixed serial fraction caps speedup no matter how many processors you add.
  2. Reevaluating Amdahl's Law — John Gustafson
    PDF·2ppCACM 1988 · DOI · PDF
    The optimistic counterpart: scale the problem with the machine and speedup grows nearly linearly — why massive parallelism is worthwhile after all.
  3. Amdahl's Law in the Multicore Era — Hill, Marty
    PDF·6ppIEEE Computer 2008 · DOI · PDF
    Applies Amdahl to chip multiprocessors, showing how symmetric, asymmetric, and dynamic core mixes change the speedup ceiling.

The walls

Where the free lunch first ran out: off-chip bandwidth, the cache misses behind it, on-chip wires, and the cost of a bigger core.

  1. Hitting the Memory Wall: Implications of the Obvious — Wulf, McKee
    PDF·5ppSIGARCH CAN 1995 · DOI · PDF
    Coined the "memory wall": with processor speed outrunning DRAM, average access time comes to dominate performance.
  2. Evaluating Associativity in CPU Caches — Hill, Smith
    PDF·19ppIEEE TC 1989 · DOI · PDF
    Source of the "three Cs" model — every miss is compulsory, capacity, or conflict — the vocabulary that turned cache design into a quantitative accounting of where the memory-wall traffic comes from.
  3. The Future of Wires — Ho, Mai, Horowitz
    PDF·15ppProc. IEEE 2001 · DOI · PDF
    As transistors shrank, wires did not get faster — global interconnect delay came to dominate, one of the physical reasons designs went multicore and tiled rather than bigger and monolithic.
  4. Coming Challenges in Microarchitecture and Architecture — Ronen, Mendelson, Lai, Lu, Pollack, Shen
    DOI·16ppProc. IEEE 2001 · DOI
    The source of Pollack's Rule — a core's performance rises only as the square root of its area — the diminishing-returns argument that pushed the field toward multicore.

The end of Dennard scaling and the power wall

When power stopped scaling with density, energy — not transistor count — became the limiter.

  1. The Case for Energy-Proportional Computing — Barroso, Hölzle
    PDF·5ppIEEE Computer 2007 · DOI · PDF
    Machines run mostly at low utilization yet burn near-peak power there; argues energy use should track load — the framing for datacenter-scale efficiency.
  2. Dark Silicon and the End of Multicore Scaling — Esmaeilzadeh, Blem, St. Amant, Sankaralingam, Burger
    PDF·12ppISCA 2011 · DOI · PDF
    Post-Dennard, the power budget forces much of a chip to stay dark at any instant — capping how many cores you can actually use.
  3. The Future of Microprocessors — Borkar, Chien
    PDF·11ppCACM 2011 · DOI · PDF
    The industry statement of the same turn: energy efficiency, not transistor count, is now the fundamental limiter.
  4. Computing's Energy Problem (and What We Can Do About It) — Mark Horowitz
    PDF·5ppISSCC 2014 · DOI · PDF
    Quantifies where the joules actually go — instruction and data movement dwarf arithmetic — motivating specialization over general-purpose cores.
  5. Razor: A Low-Power Pipeline Based on Circuit-Level Timing Speculation — Ernst, Kim, Das, Pant, Rao, Pham, Ziesler, Blaauw, Austin, Flautner, Mudge
    PDF·12ppMICRO 2003 · DOI · PDF
    Run the voltage below the worst-case margin, detect the rare timing errors, and replay — reclaiming the guardband that static voltage/frequency scaling leaves on the table.

Models for reasoning about a design

Given the limits, the back-of-the-envelope models architects actually reach for.

  1. A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW — John Little
    DOI·5ppOperations Research 1961 · DOI
    Little's Law: in steady state, occupancy equals arrival rate times latency — the identity behind sizing buffers, miss-status registers, and reorder windows, and why bandwidth and latency are two views of one number.
  2. Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures — Williams, Waterman, Patterson
    PDF·11ppCACM 2009 · DOI · PDF
    Plots attainable performance against operational intensity, so one picture shows whether a kernel is compute- or bandwidth-bound.
  3. A Mechanistic Performance Model for Superscalar Out-of-Order Processors — Eyerman, Eeckhout, Karkhanis, Smith
    PDF·37ppACM TOCS 2009 · DOI · PDF
    Interval analysis: a model that explains superscalar performance from first principles — miss events puncturing a steady-state issue rate — rather than by simulation alone.
  4. Microarchitecture Optimizations for Exploiting Memory-Level Parallelism — Chou, Fahs, Abraham
    PDF·12ppISCA 2004 · DOI · PDF
    Concurrency applied to the memory system: overlapping cache misses — not the raw miss count — sets memory-bound performance, and this quantifies which microarchitectural features actually expose that parallelism.

Design choices, quantified

The same quantitative lens applied to the field's oldest design argument — and to what comes next.

  1. The Case for the Reduced Instruction Set Computer — Patterson, Ditzel
    PDF·9ppSIGARCH CAN 1980 · DOI · PDF
    The manifesto that framed RISC vs CISC as a quantitative trade rather than a matter of taste.
  2. Power Struggles: Revisiting the RISC vs. CISC Debate on Contemporary ARM and x86 Architectures — Blem, Menon, Sankaralingam
    PDF·12ppHPCA 2013 · DOI · PDF
    Measures modern ARM and x86 parts and finds the ISA is largely irrelevant to power and performance — the debate settled with data.
  3. A New Golden Age for Computer Architecture — Hennessy, Patterson
    PDF·13ppCACM 2019 · DOI · PDF
    The synthesis: with the scaling laws spent, domain-specific architectures are where the gains now come from.
The whole arc in one breath. For decades Moore gave more transistors and Dennard gave them for free, so single cores got faster on their own. When Dennard scaling ended, power — not transistor count — became the limiter (Barroso, Borkar, Horowitz), Pollack's rule made bigger cores a bad deal, and Amdahl capped how far multicore could carry us. What's left is to spend a fixed power budget wisely: measure honestly (the iron law, geometric means, SPEC), model the bottleneck (Little's Law, roofline), and specialize the hardware to the workload (the "new golden age").

Key terms

Vocabulary used across this list.

iron law of performance — execution time = instructions × cycles-per-instruction × cycle time; every speedup acts on one of these three factors   geometric mean — the correct average for normalized ratios (SPEC scores); the arithmetic mean of ratios can reorder machines depending on the baseline chosen   Amdahl's Law — speedup is bounded by the fraction of work that stays serial, however many processors you add   Gustafson's Law — when the problem grows with the machine, achievable speedup grows nearly linearly with processor count   Moore's Law — the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles on a fixed cadence (~2 years)   Dennard scaling — as feature size shrinks, voltage and current scale with it so power density stays constant; its end is what capped clock frequency   Pollack's Rule — a core's performance grows about as the square root of its area (design complexity)   three Cs — the classification of cache misses into compulsory, capacity, and conflict   dark silicon — the fraction of a chip that must stay unpowered at any instant to fit the power budget   wire-delay scaling — as feature size shrinks, gate delay falls but global-wire delay does not, so long interconnect — not logic — bounds the clock   Little's Law — in steady state, occupancy = arrival rate × latency; the bound relating outstanding requests, throughput, and latency   memory-level parallelism (MLP) — the number of independent memory accesses (typically cache misses) a machine keeps in flight at once   operational intensity — floating-point operations performed per byte of DRAM traffic; the x-axis of the roofline model   energy-proportional — power draw that scales with utilization, ideally falling toward zero at idle
